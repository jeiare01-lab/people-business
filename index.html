Preface

Thank you

Who would have thought an HR practitioner would end up writing a book that treats payroll as a P&L?

Not me. Not when I started. HR taught me people. It took two (2) leaders to teach me what people cost, what they return, and what a company loses when nobody is counting.

To the Chief Human Resources Officer

You gave me the work before I felt ready for it. Every assignment came with room to get it wrong and a standard that did not move. That is how a practitioner gets sharpened. Not by being told, but by being trusted with something that could fail, and then being asked what I learned.

This book is full of what I learned that way. The mistakes in it are mine. The nerve to try again is yours.

To the Business Integrator

You let me see the numbers beyond HR. Before that, I knew headcount, turnover and survey scores. You showed me margin, cash, and what an empty seat costs a project by the week. And you never treated the people question as the smaller one.

Chapter 1 is what happens when an HR practitioner learns to price a resignation in pesos. The rest of the book follows from that one habit.

To both of you

The argument in these pages is yours as much as mine. An HR practitioner who can read the numbers beyond HR is not a better administrator. That practitioner is a better partner. If this book helps one leader see their people the way you taught me to see them, it will have earned its place.

James Villalon II · HR Strategic Partner · October 2026

Introduction

You already run a people business

Your company spends more on people than it admits, and reports on them less than it reports on diesel.

Open your SBU's P&L. Find the payroll line. Now find everything that rides behind it: overtime, the 13th month accrual, SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG remittances, agency fees, recruitment costs, training, separation pay, legal fees from the last labor case. Add them. That total is your people spend.

Now answer one question: who owns it?

Not who processes it. Who owns it. Who is accountable for what it returns, who explains the variance, who answers when it leaks.

In most conglomerates, nobody does. Finance pays it. HR administers it. Operations consumes it. The CEO feels it only when something breaks: a key engineer resigns mid-project, a regularization case reaches the NLRC, a successor turns out to be unready the week the incumbent retires. By then the bill is large and the cause is old.

You already run a people business. You are running it without a P&L.

Three things people are at once

CostYour largest controllable line. Every peso of payroll is a commitment made months ahead of the revenue meant to cover it.
AssetThe only one that appreciates when you invest in it and walks out the gate when you do not. No other asset on your books can resign.
RiskCompliance exposure, key-person dependency, labor disputes, succession gaps. None shows up as a line item until it becomes a loss.

A good owner manages all three at once. Most leaders manage the cost and delegate it downward as an administrative chore.

What changes when you treat it as a business

The vocabulary of HR was built for specialists. This book drops it where it can and translates it where it cannot.

HR saysThis book says
TurnoverThe resignation invoice
HiringCapital allocation, with a payback period
OnboardingTime-to-productivity, paid in full
EngagementDiscretionary effort you pay for and do not get
SuccessionContinuity insurance: premium, coverage, claim
The HR functionA business unit that earns its seat or loses it

The move is simple and uncomfortable. Stop asking whether your people practices are fair, modern or compliant. They should be, but that is the entry fee. Start asking what they return.

Practitioner's desk

If you are in HR, this book is the brief your executives wish you would give them. Every business number here maps to a metric you already track or can pull from the HRIS. Where a chapter uses a plain business term, the desk note names the HR term behind it, so you can bridge both audiences in one meeting.

The four moves

  1. Protect the downside. Stop the loss before you grow anything.
  2. Build the engine. Seats that earn, hires that pay back, pay that holds talent.
  3. Grow the asset. Capability, bench strength, succession, culture as operating discipline.
  4. Run it as a business. A people P&L, a quarterly rhythm, an HR function that earns its seat.

What this book is not

It is not a legal reference. Philippine labor law changes, and your counsel decides what applies to you. It is not a case against compassion either. The companies that price their people honestly are the ones that can afford to treat them well.

And it is not for passive reading. Every chapter ends with one page: a number to compute, a decision to make, a conversation to have this week. Skip the page and you have read an essay. Do the page and you have changed a business.

Before you turn the page

Write down what you believe one resignation costs your company. One figure, in pesos, for a mid-level employee you would regret losing. Keep it. Chapter 1 will tell you how far off you are.

Chapter 1 · Part 1, Protect the downside

The Resignation Invoice

One resignation costs about 85% of that employee's annual cash pay. Nobody has ever sent you the bill.

A Tuesday, a letter, a silence

A project engineer hands in a resignation letter on a Tuesday. He is good. He holds the site schedule in his head, the subcontractors answer his calls, and the client asks for him by name. Thirty days later he is gone.

What happens next is a composite, drawn from patterns that repeat across project-based businesses. The seat stays empty for ten (10) weeks. Two teammates absorb his work and slip their own. The replacement, hired through an agency, needs three (3) months to reach half speed. The site loses a week.

Ask anyone what this cost and the answer is a shrug, or a number that stops at the agency fee. The shrug is the problem. The invoice exists. It is split across three (3) departments and paid by none.

Three bills, three blind spots

  • Finance sees the agency fee and the final pay. It does not see the lost output.
  • Operations feels the vacancy and the delay. It does not see the recruitment cost.
  • HR counts the exit in a turnover percentage. It does not see the peso value of any of it.

Each function holds one piece. No one adds them. A cost nobody discusses is a cost nobody manages.

Practitioner's desk

The business term is the resignation invoice. The HR term is cost of turnover, usually quoted as a multiple of annual pay. Published multiples vary widely by level and industry, so do not borrow one. Compute yours from your own vacancy days, agency spend and ramp-up data. Your CFO will trust a figure built from their own margin, not a benchmark from a journal.

  • Turnover rate = separations ÷ average headcount. Track it, then split it into regretted and non-regretted.
  • Pull from the HRIS: resignation dates, requisition open and close dates, agency invoices, start dates.

Build the invoice

The formula is plain: net vacancy loss, plus replacement, plus ramp-up, plus team drag, plus delay, plus exit processing, plus the time and training you spend on the way. Where I will push back on you

The wrong lesson is "drive turnover to zero." Zero turnover means you cannot remove poor performers and you are not refreshing skills. Some exits are the best news of the year.

The right lesson is narrower. Split exits into regretted and non-regretted. Price the regretted ones in critical seats. That is the number that deserves an owner, a target, and a place in your quarterly review.

And resist the comfort of the exit interview. People give you the polite reason. The real reason is usually a manager, a pay gap against the market, or a career path they could not see. Those are three (3) different fixes. Parts 2 and 3 take each in turn.

Practitioner's desk

Before you present this to an exec team, expect three challenges and prepare for each.

  • "Where did the margin figure come from?" Get it from Finance, in writing, before the meeting.
  • "Some of these people were leaving anyway." Agree, and show only the regretted exits.
  • "Vacancy saves us salary." It does, and the calculator already subtracts it.

Then go back to the figure you wrote down before this chapter. Mark the gap. The gap is your first Rock.

Chapter 2 · Part 1, Protect the downside

Labor Cost Leakage

Payroll pays for work. It also pays for the gaps around the work, and nobody audits the gaps.

A variance nobody could explain

A general manager opens the monthly payroll and sees it running 6% over budget. She asks three (3) people why. Finance says overtime. Operations says the project required it. HR says headcount is within plan.

All three are right, and the money is still gone. Each function explained its own column. Nobody asked how much of the overtime was a purchase and how much was a symptom. This chapter gives you that split.

Five doors the money leaves through

  1. Overtime nobody planned. Overtime bought to hit a client deadline is spend. Overtime caused by poor scheduling, late materials or one understaffed shift is a leak.
  2. Absence nobody follows up. Every unscheduled absence is a paid day with no output, multiplied by headcount.
  3. Idle and rework time. Paid hours spent waiting for materials, instructions or approvals, or redoing work. The ranks pay for management's gaps, and it lands in payroll.
  4. Payroll errors that never come back. Duplicate allowances, overpayments, separated employees still paid. Small each month, permanent in total.
  5. Heads nobody approved. Positions filled above the approved manning plan, or kept after the work ended.
Practitioner's desk

These map to numbers you can pull without a new system.

  • Overtime ratio = overtime hours ÷ regular hours, by department. Source: timekeeping and the payroll register.
  • Absenteeism rate = unscheduled absence days ÷ scheduled workdays. Source: DTR and leave records.
  • Headcount against the approved manning plan, by position. Source: HRIS and the approved manning plan.
  • Payroll variance lines Finance has stopped explaining. Ask for the last six months.

Where I will push back on you

Not all of this is recoverable, and the book will not pretend otherwise. Overtime during a real crunch is a purchase. Some absence is illness, and you do not want people at work sick. Some idle time is the price of a project that depends on weather and delivery trucks.

Apply three (3) tests to every line before you call it a leak: Was it planned? Was it approved? Did it buy output? A yes to all three is spend. A no to any is leakage. Set your recoverable share from those tests, not from hope.

And do not start with the ranks. Most leakage is created upstream, in scheduling, purchasing and approvals. If the first leak you plug is a supervisor's overtime and the second is a delayed material order, you have found the real owner of the first.

Practitioner's desk

Agree the recoverable share with Finance before you present. A CFO who sets the 40% with you will defend it in the room. One who first hears it from the screen will challenge every line.

The one-page tool

Number to compute

Leakage as a percentage of payroll, for one SBU.

  1. Take twelve (12) months of the payroll register and the manning plan.
  2. Mark each overtime line planned or unplanned, approved or not.
  3. Pull absence days from the DTR and set a target with the operations head.
  4. Ask supervisors to estimate idle and rework hours for one typical week. Multiply, then halve it to stay conservative.
  5. List payroll errors found this year and what came back.
  6. Count heads above plan. Enter everything in the calculator.
Decision to make

Which one (1) leak gets plugged in the next thirty (30) days? Name the leak and its owner. The owner is rarely HR.

Conversation to have this week

Sit with Finance and payroll. Ask: "Which three (3) variance lines did we stop explaining?" Write down the answers. They are your starting list.

Chapter 3 · Part 1, Protect the downside

Compliance Is Exposure

Every obligation you have missed is a liability with a clock on it, and the clock is already running.

Thirty-five people and a fourteen-month contract

A manufacturing plant keeps thirty-five (35) workers on contractual terms. They run the line every day. Some have been there fourteen (14) months. Nobody decided to ignore the rules. Each renewal was signed to avoid a decision.

This is a composite, and it repeats across the country. One complaint to DOLE, or one inspection, and thirty-five (35) quiet renewals become thirty-five (35) claims. Regularizing them on schedule would have cost a fraction of what the claims cost to defend.

Why this belongs on the P&L

Finance already knows how to treat a lawsuit it might lose. It estimates the likelihood, estimates the amount, and decides whether the number belongs in the books. Compliance gaps deserve the same discipline. Most conglomerates treat them as paperwork instead, so the exposure is never summed and never owned.

Five exposures every Philippine conglomerate carries

  1. Wage and benefit shortfalls. Holiday, overtime and night differential pay, regional wage orders, the 13th month, service incentive leave. Each is small per person and multiplied across a workforce and several years.
  2. Overdue regularization. People kept on contractual or probationary terms in roles the business needs every day.
  3. Contracting arrangements. Workers supplied by a contractor. If the arrangement fails the rules on legitimate contracting, the principal can be treated as the employer.
  4. Statutory remittances. SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG contributions paid late, short or not at all.
  5. Terminations and disputes. Cases at DOLE or the NLRC, and complaints you can see coming. An illegal dismissal finding can carry back wages and either reinstatement or separation pay in lieu.
Practitioner's desk

Build an exposure register. One row per exposure: who is affected, the evidence, the amount, an owner, a date of last review. The likelihood is a judgment, so put it through your labor counsel before it reaches an executive.

  • Present both numbers: the probability-weighted exposure and the worst case. Executives remember the second one.
  • Ask Finance whether any item belongs in a provision. That decision is theirs and their auditors', not yours.
  • Rules change. Every legal statement in this chapter is for orientation. Counsel confirms what applies to you.

Chapter 4 · Part 1, Protect the downside

Key People and the Ranks Dynamic

Two risks sit at opposite ends of the org chart and cost money the same way: the few people you cannot replace, and the many whose silence you mistake for contentment.

The Friday test

Pick any business unit. Ask one question: if this person resigned on Friday, what stops on Monday?

If the answer is "nothing," the seat is a normal seat. If the answer is a production line, a client relationship, a permit, a signature or a schedule only one head holds, you have found a single point of failure. Most leaders can name two or three (3) of them. Few can name the seventh, and the seventh is usually the one who leaves.

A composite: a plant manager is the only one who knows the sequence that keeps a line running between maintenance windows. Nothing is written down. He is offered a better job. The line runs at 60% for five (5) months while his replacement relearns what he carried in his head.

Price the people you cannot replace

A key-person exit costs more than an ordinary exit because the vacancy is long and the margin at stake is large. The register below prices each seat with three (3) numbers: months to rebuild what the person held, margin at risk each of those months, and the chance of losing them in the next twelve (12).

Practitioner's desk

The HR terms are criticality (impact if the seat is vacant) and flight risk (likelihood of exit). Plot each key person on both. Do not trust a manager's guess at flight risk alone. Run a stay conversation with each person and note what they say keeps them, and what would make them look.

  • Criticality inputs: unique knowledge, relationships, authority, scarcity in the labor market.
  • Flight risk signals: pay position against the market, time since the last promotion or real change in scope, a new manager, a recruiter's recent interest.

Where I will push back on you

A key person is also a management failure. If one head holds the sequence, the permit relationship or the client, the company allowed it. The cheapest retention plan is a second person who can do the job at 70%, and a written page of what the first person knows.

Retention pay has a second price. When the ranks learn that a select few received a retention award, the next grievance carries it. Do it, and be ready to explain why it protects everyone's jobs.

The gate between the ranks and management

The ranks are rank-and-file employees: the people who run the lines, the sites, the vessels and the counters. The gate is the space between them and the people who decide. It costs money when information crosses it late, when a grievance has no short path to someone who can fix it, and when supervisors are the only interface and have neither the authority nor the time.

A composite: a night shift raises a problem with schedule changes. It goes to a supervisor, then waits five (5) weeks. By week six (6), it is a slowdown. By week eight (8), counsel is involved. The first conversation, held in week one (1), would have cost one supervisor's afternoon.

Practitioner's desk

Track grievances as data, not anecdote: number raised, age of each open item, time to first response, time to resolution, repeat topics by site and shift. A site with a long first-response time is a dispute waiting for a date.

  • Where a union and a collective bargaining agreement exist, the agreement and its grievance machinery govern. Counsel sets the line between a conversation and a labor matter.
  • Where there is no union, build a short, visible path anyway: a named person, a response time you can keep.

Chapter 5 · Part 2, Build the engine

Seats and Hires

A seat that costs more than it earns or protects is a subsidy, and most org charts carry several.

Fourteen heads became twenty-two

A department grows from fourteen (14) to twenty-two (22) people in two years. Each hire was approved on its own merits: a manager was stretched, a deadline was close. Nobody asked what the department would produce with eight (8) more people. Revenue per head fell for six (6) straight quarters, and the cause had no owner because no single hire looked wrong. This is a composite.

Every seat has a job

A seat does one of three (3) things. It earns revenue or margin. It protects something that would otherwise be lost: safety, compliance, quality, a client. Or it enables others to do the first two.

Write one sentence for every seat: this seat earns, protects or enables ______, and we will know by ______. If you cannot finish it, the seat needs redesign before it needs a hire.

A requisition is a capital request

Treat a hire the way you treat a machine: money out now, value in later, with a payback period and a hurdle. The inputs are few. What the seat is worth in a year. What the person costs, loaded. What the hire costs. How long until full speed.

Practitioner's desk

Time-to-fill and cost-per-hire measure your process. Quality of hire measures the result: performance and retention at month twelve (12). Track which sources produce hires who reach month twelve (12), then spend there.

Add the EOS GWC test to the requisition: does the candidate get it, want it, and have the capacity for the seat? Write the answer down before the offer, not after.

The starting figures describe an engineer-level seat and are illustrative. Use the requisition you are about to approve.

With the example, the seat pays back in month six (6) and returns about ₱1.4 million over thirty-six (36) months. Now set the ramp to eight (8) months at 30% output. Payback moves to month fifteen (15), past a twelve-month hurdle. A slow ramp, not the salary, is what makes a hire expensive.

Where I will push back on you

Payback math is built for seats that earn. For seats that protect or enable, enter the avoided cost as the value: the exit cost from Chapter 1, the exposure from Chapter 3. Do not feed it revenue the seat never touches.

Do not freeze every requisition either. The cheapest way to lose a good manager is to keep adding to their load. And a referral is not free: the bonus is a cost, and so is the standing of the person who referred a poor fit.

The one-page tool

Number to compute

Payback in months for your next three (3) requisitions. Enter each in the calculator and write the three numbers on one line.

Decision to make

Freeze or redesign the requisition with the longest payback. Name who tells the hiring manager.

Conversation to have this week

Ask the hiring manager: "What will this person produce by day ninety (90) that we can count?" If the answer is vague, the requisition is not ready.

Chapter 6 · Part 2, Build the engine

Onboarding and Pay

The gap between a start date and first real output is paid in full, and so is the pay gap that makes your best people look elsewhere.

The new hire who out-earned the veteran

A new engineer joins at ₱48,000 a month. A three-year incumbent in the same grade earns ₱49,000. The difference leaks within weeks, the way it always does. Two strong performers, one of them paid ₱51,000 and meant to be protected, update their resumes. This is a composite, and most conglomerates have a version of it.

Onboarding is time-to-productivity

Day one is not the start of productivity. It is the start of the payroll. Define productive as the day a new hire produces something countable without supervision, then count the days.

Everything in onboarding either shortens that number or does not. A named buddy, a first assignment by day three (3), tools ready on arrival, a manager who sets the first thirty (30) days in writing: these matter because they shave days.

With the example, thirty (30) hires a year at a three-month ramp cost about ₱2.4 million in paid-but-missing output. Cut the ramp by 30% and about ₱730,000 comes back, roughly twenty-seven (27) days per hire.

Pay is the price of talent

Three (3) numbers tell you whether your pay holds talent. The compa-ratio is pay divided by the midpoint of the band. The compression index is the pay of your newest hire divided by the pay of a tenured incumbent. The premium for tenure is the gap you intend between them.

When new hires nearly match veterans, the veterans find out. Then they price their own exit.

Practitioner's desk

Review the spread by grade, not company-wide. Check every offer against current incumbents in the same grade before it goes out. If your salary structure is being redesigned, this is the first thing to test against the new bands.

  • Compa-ratio = pay ÷ band midpoint. Below 90% flags a retention risk. Above 110% flags a band problem or a promotion that is due.
  • Pay differences for similar work need a documented reason. Counsel reviews any pay-equity question.

With the example, new hires earn 98% of what a three-year incumbent earns. Restoring an 8% premium for twenty-five (25) incumbents costs about ₱923,000 a year. One avoided exit is worth about ₱541,000 by the Chapter 1 rule. The fix pays for itself if it prevents about 1.7 exits.

Where I will push back on you

Do not raise everyone. Start with the seats in your Chapter 4 register and the grades where compression is worst. And do not treat pay as the whole answer. People leave over managers and paths at least as often as over ₱2,000 a month. The calculator tells you what a fix costs against what exits cost. It does not tell you why people leave. Ask them.

The one-page tool

Number to compute

The compression index for your three (3) largest grades, and days to productive for your last ten (10) hires.

Decision to make

Which grade is corrected first, by how much, and by when. Attach the cost and the exits it must prevent.

Conversation to have this week

Ask someone hired in the last ninety (90) days: "What took longest before you could do your job alone?" Write the answer on the onboarding checklist.

Chapter 7 · Part 2, Build the engine

Performance Per Peso

Appraisal is paperwork. Performance is output per peso, and the two rarely agree.

Forty-five percent exceeded expectations

At year-end, 45% of a unit's people are rated "exceeds expectations." The unit met one (1) target of five (5). Nobody lied. The ratings were written against effort, goodwill and the supervisor's wish to avoid a hard conversation. This is a composite.

The unit economics of a head

Every unit has an input, its loaded payroll, and an output: revenue, margin, tons shipped, units handed over, vessel turns. Divide the second by the first. That is output per peso. It is crude and it is honest.

Track it every quarter. A rising number means the unit gets more from the same pay. A falling number means growth is being paid for in headcount.

Ratings against results

Put two numbers side by side: the share of people rated at the top, and the share of unit targets actually met. A gap above fifteen (15) points means the rating scale is measuring something other than results. It is rarely dishonesty. Goals were vague, supervisors had no calibration, and the appraisal came once a year.

EOS gives you a cleaner contract. A Rock is done or not done, for ninety (90) days, with one owner. Rocks are the best evidence of delivery you will get.

Practitioner's desk
  • Run calibration before ratings are final. Ask for evidence behind every top rating.
  • Track the rating distribution by unit and by supervisor. A supervisor whose whole team is "exceeds" is a data point.
  • Goal quality: specific, measurable, dated. Reject goals that read as activities.
  • Three (3) conversations a year beat one form. The appraisal workflow is a prime automation target (Chapter 12).

With the example, output per peso rose from ₱3.59 to ₱3.75, while ratings run twenty-five (25) points ahead of results. A 5% gain in output per peso at the same payroll is worth about ₱9 million.

Where I will push back on you

Output per peso punishes support functions whose output is hard to price. For them, use cost-to-serve and service levels. And do not answer a rating gap by forcing a curve. That trades honesty for arithmetic. Fix the goals first, then calibrate.

The one-page tool

Number to compute

Output per peso of payroll for three (3) units, this year against last, plus the rating gap for each.

Decision to make

Which top rating will you stop giving without evidence? Announce the evidence rule before the next cycle.

Conversation to have this week

Hold one honest one-on-one with the person you rated highest last year. Ask: "What did I miss that you did, and what did you do that did not matter?"

Chapter 8 · Part 3, Grow the asset

Capability and Bench

Training is an expense or an investment, depending on where it points.

Sixty courses, no Rocks

A company spends ₱6 million on training in a year, across sixty (60) courses. Asked which courses served a Rock, HR can name four (4). Meanwhile two (2) senior roles open and no internal candidate qualifies. This is a composite.

Point the money at a gap

List your top three (3) Rocks. For each, name the capability the people doing it do not yet have. That is the gap. Training that closes a named gap is an investment. Training that fills a calendar is an expense.

The bench has three depths

For each critical role, ask who could step in now, who within twelve (12) months, who within thirty-six (36), and for which roles nobody. A list of names is a wish. A bench is a list of names with evidence of readiness and a development plan against the gap.

A career lattice makes the bench visible to the people on it. It maps lateral and upward moves by role, so people can see how to grow without leaving.

Practitioner's desk
  • Build a skills matrix by role family, then an Individual Development Plan for every identified successor, with an owner and dates.
  • Write readiness criteria down. "Ready now" means the person has done the role's work at a stretch assignment.
  • Handle a nine-box with care. A label such as "high potential" sticks to people and to those left out.
  • Review the bench quarterly. The HRIS should hold the data.

With the example, twenty percent (20%) of critical roles have a ready-now successor, and a quarter have none. Of ₱6 million in training spend, only ₱1.8 million points at a named gap.

Where I will push back on you

"Ready" is evidence, not impression. A successor who does not know they are named, or does not want the role, is not coverage. And do not mark someone ready now because the incumbent leaves next month. That is a vacancy with a hopeful label.

The one-page tool

Number to compute

Ready-now coverage of your critical roles, and the share of training spend aimed at a named gap.

Decision to make

Which one (1) role gets a bench built first? Pick the one with the largest number from Chapter 4.

Conversation to have this week

Tell one identified successor what you see in them. Then ask: "What would you need to be ready, and do you want this?"

Chapter 9 · Part 3, Grow the asset

Succession as Insurance

Succession is insurance. You pay a premium now so a vacancy does not become a claim later.

The heir who was not ready

The long-time head of a business unit in a family-rooted group prepares to retire. The heir apparent is a family member with a strong name and thin operating experience. The senior professional manager is trusted by the ranks and not by the family. For nine (9) months an interim holds both seats. The unit loses two (2) clients and a year of planning. This is a composite.

Premium, coverage, claim

The premium is what you spend developing successors. The coverage is the share of critical roles with a ready-now successor. The claim is what a vacancy costs when it happens: months without a successor times the margin at stake.

Two successions, not one

A family-rooted conglomerate has two (2). One is the succession of ownership and governance. It belongs to the owners, the board and the family. The other is the succession of management roles. It belongs to the CEO and HR.

A plan that mixes them fails both. Management succession stalls while it waits for an ownership decision nobody will make.

Where a succession plan fails an executive review

  • No emergency successor for the top three (3) seats if someone leaves tomorrow.
  • A successor who is a copy of the incumbent.
  • No timeline, no development plan, no budget.
  • The successor was never asked.
  • No plan B if the successor leaves.
  • The incumbent is not part of the conversation.
Practitioner's desk
  • Write an Individual Development Plan for each successor: the gap, the stretch assignment, the sponsor, the dates.
  • Keep an emergency coverage list for the top roles, separate from the development slate.
  • Review readiness in the quarterly cycle. Send the slate to the board once a year.
  • Governance instruments, such as a family constitution or shareholder arrangements, are for counsel and the owners.

With the example, lifting ready-now coverage from 20% to 70% across forty (40) roles cuts the expected annual vacancy cost from about ₱4.0 million to ₱2.0 million. The premium is ₱1.8 million, so year one nets about ₱0.2 million. The calculator does not count the retention value of developing people. Treat year one as the floor.

Where I will push back on you

The weakest part of most plans is the incumbent conversation, not the slate. An incumbent who feels replaced slows the plan down. And do not let the plan live in HR. It needs a board-level owner and a date.

The chance of a vacancy is also a judgment, and it is not the same for every role. Raise it for incumbents near retirement. A flat average hides the roles that matter.

The one-page tool

Number to compute

Ready-now coverage and expected annual claim for the critical roles in one SBU.

Decision to make

Which one (1) succession gap goes to the board? Bring a named role, a cost, and a date.

Conversation to have this week

Sit with the incumbent of the role that matters most. Ask: "What do you want your last year in this seat to build?"

Chapter 10 · Part 3, Grow the asset

Culture and the Conglomerate Question

Culture is what people do when nobody supervises them, and what it costs when they do something else.

Three values on every wall

A company prints three (3) values on every wall. In one quarter its leaders make forty (40) major decisions. Asked afterward, they can name six (6) where a value changed the outcome. The other thirty-four (34) were decided on cost, speed and habit. This is a composite.

A value only counts if it changes a decision

Ask of each value: when did this cost us something? A value that never costs anything is decoration. Check three (3) places. Decisions: did a value change or settle the outcome. Promotions: was behavior against the values assessed in writing. The weekly meeting: does the L10 agenda surface a values conflict, or only numbers?

In EOS the core values sit in the VTO. The discipline is to use them to hire, to review and to part ways.

With the example, a value touched 15% of decisions and 28% of promotions. That is a company with values on the wall and not yet in the room.

The conglomerate question

Who does what, Shared Services or the SBU? A working rule: centralize what is the same everywhere and transactional: payroll processing, statutory filings, the HRIS, policy templates. Embed what depends on the business: workforce planning, employee relations, succession, onboarding on the line.

Then decide who pays, charge-back or allocation, in advance, with service levels written down.

Practitioner's desk
  • Publish a service catalog: what Shared Services provides, how fast, at what cost.
  • Measure cost-to-serve per employee for both models. Include the manager hours spent on forms, or the shared model will look cheaper than it is.
  • Review the arrangement each quarter with the SBU heads. A resentful SBU head is your best source of data.

With the example, Shared Services costs ₱9,000 per employee and embedded HR ₱12,000. Add 300 manager hours a month on HR forms, about ₱2.9 million a year, and the true shared cost is ₱10,440 per employee.

Where I will push back on you

Centralizing to save money often moves the cost to managers, where nobody counts it. And culture cannot be centralized. Shared Services can publish the values. Only managers can enact them on a Tuesday afternoon.

The one-page tool

Number to compute

Your value-citation rate for last quarter, and the true cost per employee under your HR model.

Decision to make

Name one habit you will enforce this quarter and one you will stop excusing.

Conversations to have this week

Praise one behavior by name, in public, where others can hear it.

Then sit with an SBU head who resents the center. Ask: "What does Shared Services cost you that we do not count?"

Chapter 11 · Part 4, Run it as a business

The People P&L and the Quarterly Rhythm

A number with no owner and no rhythm is a report. Give it both and it becomes a P&L.

Forty pages of finance, one slide of headcount

A CEO's monthly pack has forty (40) pages on revenue, cost and cash. One slide shows headcount. Nobody argues with headcount, because nobody explains it. The largest controllable line in the business gets one slide and no owner. This is a composite.

Eight numbers

A people P&L needs few numbers, each with an owner:

  1. People spend as a share of revenue.
  2. Revenue per head.
  3. People spend per head.
  4. The regretted-exit bill (Chapter 1).
  5. Leakage as a share of people spend (Chapter 2).
  6. Weighted compliance exposure (Chapter 3).
  7. Ready-now successor coverage (Chapters 8 and 9).
  8. Revenue per peso of people spend.

With the example, people spend is 15.0% of revenue and each head carries ₱2.75 million. The three losses you can name, exits, leakage and exposure, total ₱22.9 million. That is 13.9% of everything the company spends on people, and none of it was on a page last year.

The quarterly rhythm

Put these numbers on the same cadence as sales and cash. Use the EOS meetings you already run.

CadenceWhat goes on the page
Weekly (L10, scorecard)Headcount against plan, open requisitions, regretted exits to date, unscheduled absence rate, age of the oldest open grievance. People problems join the IDS list like any other issue.
Quarterly (Rocks)People-dependent Rocks, a refreshed exposure register, a bench review, pay compression checked for one grade.
AnnualVTO check, pay review, succession slate to the board, appraisal and development cycle.
Practitioner's desk
  • Write a data dictionary: for each metric, the formula, the source system, the owner and the refresh date. Define it once.
  • Manual chasing of workforce reports is the case for finishing the HR dashboard. Connect the HRIS, payroll and timekeeping.
  • Clean data first. Start with the five numbers you can source cleanly and mark the other three as estimates on the page.

Where I will push back on you

Eight is the limit. A people page with thirty metrics is a data dump. And a dashboard fed by bad data is worse than none, because it carries false authority. If a figure is an estimate, say so on the page in plain words.

The one-page tool

Number to compute

The eight numbers for one SBU, on one page. Use the calculator, then replace each example figure with a sourced one.

Decision to make

Which metric gets a named owner by Friday? Start with the largest loss.

Conversation to have this week

Show the page to your CEO. Say: "These are the numbers I propose we review every quarter."

Chapter 12 · Part 4, Run it as a business

Automate, Rebuild the Function, First 90 Days

Hand the repetitive work to systems, so people can do the work only people can do.

Strategy on weekends

An HR team of twelve (12) spends most of its month on signatures, chasing appraisals and assembling workforce reports by hand. Strategy happens on weekends. The leader is a strategic partner in title and a clerk in the calendar. This is a composite, and it is common.

What to automate

  • Signatures and approvals through a workflow with an audit trail.
  • Goal-setting and appraisal routing, with reminders that nobody has to send by hand.
  • Policy acknowledgments.
  • Workforce reports straight from the HRIS.
  • First drafts of policies, job descriptions and communications from AI tools, always reviewed by a person.

Two guardrails. Employee personal data is governed by the Data Privacy Act, so counsel and your data protection officer decide what may enter any tool. And any decision that affects someone's employment gets a human review. Fix the process before you automate it. An automated mess is the same mess, faster.

With the example, a twelve-person team returns about 5,000 hours a year, worth about ₱2.3 million, or roughly 2.7 full-time equivalents. Against a ₱6 million function that protected or recovered ₱9 million, each peso of HR cost returns ₱1.50.

The function that earns its seat

A people function does three (3) jobs. Protect: compliance, employee relations, the exposure register. Build: hiring, pay, performance, capability, succession. Run: analytics, systems, the people P&L. One team doing all three at once does none well.

Decide what HR stops doing: forms nobody reads, reports nobody uses, approvals that add days and no judgment. Hire for judgment, not for forms.

Practitioner's desk
  • Report your own function's cost per employee and its return per peso, using the same page you gave the executives.
  • Common structures: a central team with SBU-based partners, or a central team with a shared transaction desk. Pick on cost-to-serve, not on fashion.
  • List your tasks by who must do them: a person with judgment, a system, or nobody. Start the automation with the second list.

The first ninety days

  1. Days 1 to 30, measure. Compute three (3) numbers: the cost of one regretted exit (Chapter 1), leakage as a share of payroll (Chapter 2), and your single points of failure (Chapter 4).
  2. Days 31 to 60, fix. Plug one leak, close one exposure, name one deputy.
  3. Days 61 to 90, install. Build the one-page people P&L, put the people numbers on the L10, and set the first people Rock for next quarter.

Where I will push back on you

Do not launch twelve initiatives. The book is sequenced for a reason: Part 1 before Part 2. A company that hires faster before it stops leaking is pouring water into a bucket with a hole in it.

The one-page tool

Number to compute

Your three (3) numbers from days 1 to 30. Write them on a card and keep it where you plan the quarter.

Decision to make

Your first people Rock, in one sentence, with one owner and one date.

Conversation to have this week

Go to the person who owns the largest number on your page. Ask: "What would you change first if this were yours alone to fix?"

Back to the first question: who owns it? In ninety (90) days you should answer by name.
